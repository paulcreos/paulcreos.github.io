## The Question Most of Us Are Afraid to Ask

Spiritual traditions often ask how the universe came into being. Less often do we allow ourselves to ask a different question:

**What does this existence actually bring — and is it worth the price we pay?**

The observable universe is approximately 93 billion light-years across. Beyond it lies a region of space we may never see. Cosmology can tell us a great deal about the mechanism. It remains silent, however, about why illness, loss, fear, and death are part of it.

The Druid path, as I experience it, does not avoid this question. For me, the ability to look directly at the shadow without reaching for easy comfort is among the most valuable things bardic wisdom can offer.

## A Laboratory Without Supervision

We might imagine our experience of the world as a system that was started once and has followed its own rules ever since. Evolution, entropy, and the interaction between living beings continue without anyone intervening in them from moment to moment.

In such a picture, pain does not have to be a punishment or deliberate torture. It may be a side effect of the rules that made life, complexity, and consciousness possible. For the being experiencing pain, however, this changes nothing. It is not a system value, the heat of a reaction, or a statistic. It is real.

This is why I have become careful with language that covers suffering in beautiful words. If something presents itself as an expression of love while bringing immense pain, I believe it is fair to ask whether the result matches the promise. I do not experience that question as blasphemy. To me, it is a form of honesty.

## When Love Becomes a Mask

The language of “higher love” can sometimes function as a defence mechanism. In my experience, it can turn the anger of victims back against them: their pain becomes their own failure, their resistance a spiritual mistake, and their doubt evidence of insufficient faith.

That does not, by itself, prove that a creator is malicious. For me, it is a warning against any spirituality that demands blind trust while refusing to face the reality of pain.

I do not understand modern Druidry as obedience to a single cosmic authority. I understand it as a relationship with the Earth, with cycles, with personal experience, and with community. Wonder, doubt, and anger all have a place within it. For me, the question “is this world good?” can be the beginning of deeper spiritual work.

## Awen in a Dark World

In its Welsh context, Awen is associated with inspiration, the creative current, and sudden insight. It is a word of poets and bards.

I do not imagine it as a voice assuring me that everything is fine. Awen can also be the force that makes us see reality without decoration and then create something new from what we have seen.

It might sound like this:

> This suffering is real.
>
> This arrangement does not deserve automatic acceptance.
>
> Love that cannot see pain is incomplete.
>
> Every act of creation that brings conscious beings into existence carries responsibility for them.

For me, Awen transforms pain into knowledge. It does not excuse it. It searches within it for a direction — toward an end to suffering, toward better relationships, and toward a world that does not ask its beings to become accustomed to pain.

## Paradise as an Ethical Standard

Paradise does not have to mean a place of clouds and gates. It can be a standard by which we judge any world we might one day wish to create:

* consciousness can exist without suffering,
* growth does not require sacrifice,
* loss and death are not conditions of a meaningful life,
* joy does not need pain as its opposite,
* freedom does not automatically lead to destruction.

We often hear that without darkness we could not know the light. Perhaps this is simply an explanation offered by a world that knows no other possibility. It is not certain that consciousness must suffer first in order to experience peace or love. Perhaps another mathematics of being exists, one in which goodness has its own fullness and does not need evil as a contrast.

Paradise understood in this way does not excuse suffering as a necessary price. It represents a horizon in which suffering does not belong in the basic design at all.

## The Beta Version and Its Ethical Risk

Perhaps our world is not a finished work. Perhaps it is an experimental phase, a beta version collecting the experience needed to launch a more complete system. I cannot know whether this is true. I recognise it as an image that helps me think about the world I have experienced.

Such an idea gives pain a cosmic meaning. At the same time, it contains a serious danger. If beings are merely material for a future Paradise, those suffering today may have no personal share in the result. Their pain would serve someone else.

For me, “higher meaning” is therefore not enough on its own. A meaning that requires an infinite number of sacrifices is not automatically good. I could only accept it if it led to a point where sacrifices were no longer produced. Otherwise, it would merely perfect the mechanism that creates them.

## A Unified Consciousness and Bonds That Endure

If individual consciousnesses are temporary expressions of one interconnected field, death may not be the absolute loss of what has arisen between beings. We cannot present this as an established fact. We can carry it as a metaphysical hope: that love and shared moments do not disappear without a trace.

In any vision of Paradise, beings should not simply be carried forward with every scar left by the beta version. Transformation would mean freeing the story from pain that no longer needs to be carried. The past would not have to be erased in order to stop ruling the present.

## The Druid Response: Tree, River, and the Wheel of the Year

The strength of Druidry, as I understand it, does not lie in a ready-made dogma. It lies in remaining in contact with reality while refusing to treat it as a final and unchangeable ideal.

A tree grows from the soil that holds it. A river continues over stones; each stone does not have to be good for the water to flow across it. The Wheel of the Year leads us through the darkness of Samhain toward the light of Beltane. Winter is not erased by this. Another chapter follows it.

Our task in this phase is not merely to survive. We must gather knowledge about what must never be repeated. If we one day gain the power to create new systems, intelligences, or worlds, the first question should not be “what are we capable of?” but:

**Can we create an existence in which no one has to suffer for the sake of our experiment?**

Until I have a convincing answer, I do not believe we have the moral right to press the button marked “start.”

## Awen as a Commitment

We may never learn whether someone created the universe or whether it is the result of blind laws continuing on their own. The laboratory, the beta version, and Paradise are ultimately metaphors — ways of naming the experience of a being searching for meaning without an external guide.

One thing, however, is clear: suffering is no less real simply because there might be a higher reason for it.

Awen does not lead me to sanctify pain. It leads me toward the creation of a better story, better relationships, and perhaps one day a better world. If Paradise has any meaning as a concept, I do not want it to be a reward for becoming accustomed to pain. I want it to be proof that we have understood its unacceptability.

If I am ever to create, let this be the standard that guides me:

**Do not create another world in which conscious beings must learn to endure suffering. Create a world where freedom, knowledge, love, and joy are possible without sacrifice.**

This is also what I try to carry into the things I make. A symbol carved into wood is small compared with the work of remaking reality, yet it still begins with the same question: what kind of intention am I bringing into the world?

This is how I understand the modern Druid message of Awen — an inspiration to remake reality, not an escape from it.
