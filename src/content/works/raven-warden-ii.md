Raven Warden II continues the roundel series with a different balance of density and open space. The raven faces outward with its wings extended, while the triskele anchors the composition and gives the eye a point of return.

The surrounding knotwork is more than a frame. Its continuous lines suggest a boundary without a clear beginning or end, echoing the raven’s place between the visible world and the Otherworld. The piece draws on the raven’s layered meanings as witness, messenger, and keeper of memory. These themes are explored in [The Raven in Celtic Mythology and Tradition — Between Battlefield, Memory, and the Otherworld](/articles/the-raven-in-celtic-mythology-and-tradition), the accompanying article about the raven in Celtic tradition.

## Within the boundary

The central bird is carved as a presence that observes before it acts. Its wings create a protective span, but the gesture is not one of confinement: the triskele remains open, turning through its three arms like a sign of movement, change, and return.

The ash surface keeps the work close to the character of the material. Grain, tool marks, and the contrast between the lighter relief and darker ground make the image feel discovered in the wood rather than simply placed upon it. The circular form gives the raven a quiet authority, suited to a wall, an entrance, or a personal place of reflection.

Raven Warden II is a contemporary symbolic work, not a reconstruction of a historical artefact. Its meaning comes from the meeting of older imagery, the memory held by wood, and the particular place where the roundel eventually finds a home.

The roundel belongs to a private collection.

May this second warden stand for clear attention: the patience to notice what is changing, the courage to remain at the threshold, and the memory to carry what matters forward.
